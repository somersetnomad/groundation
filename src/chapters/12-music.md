<div class="eyebrow">Wareika Hill</div>
<h2 class="h-mid">The drummer who <span class="acc-y">changed</span> the record</h2>

Before 1960, Rasta drumming lived in camps and yards, far from Jamaica's studios. Count Ossie's camp in the Wareika Hills was where that changed. Jazz musicians climbed the hill to play with his drummers, and Prince Buster brought the drummers down to record.

By the early 1970s Ossie's group, the Mystic Revelation of Rastafari, had made *Grounation*, a triple album that set Nyabinghi drums alongside jazz horns and spoken reasoning. Count Ossie died in 1976, but nearly every Rasta-influenced record since carries some of his pulse.

<div class="eyebrow">7 July 1977</div>
<h2 class="h-mid">When a song <span class="acc-r">stopped</span> a city</h2>

Culture's Joseph Hill wrote "Two Sevens Clash" about a prophecy attributed to Marcus Garvey: that chaos would come when the sevens met, on the seventh day of the seventh month of 1977. The song was everywhere that year. On the day itself, much of Kingston stayed home and shut its doors.

It's a reminder that, for many Jamaicans, roots reggae was never just entertainment. It carried prophecy, history and warning, and people listened.

<div class="eyebrow">A note on listening</div>
<h2 class="h-mid">Hear it as it was <span class="acc-g">meant</span></h2>

Much Rasta music was made to be heard loud, together, on a sound system, not alone through earbuds. If you can, find a roots or dub sound system night near you. Many still treat the dance as a gathering with a spiritual purpose, and the bass is meant to be felt as much as heard.
