The ground had been prepared for decades. **Ethiopianism**, the idea that Africa and its peoples held a central place in God's plan, ran through Black churches across the Caribbean and the Americas. It rested on Psalm 68:31: "Princes shall come out of Egypt; Ethiopia shall soon stretch out her hands unto God."

Marcus Garvey's Universal Negro Improvement Association, founded in Kingston in 1914, gave that hope a mass movement and a flag of red, black and green.

<div class="date-card"><span class="tabular">2.11.1930</span><b>King of Kings, Lord of Lords, Conquering Lion of the Tribe of Judah</b><em>Addis Ababa</em></div>

</div><div class="prose read">

On **2 November 1930**, Ras Tafari Makonnen was crowned in Addis Ababa as Haile Selassie I, "King of Kings, Lord of Lords, Conquering Lion of the Tribe of Judah". Newspapers carried photographs of European dignitaries bowing before a Black monarch. For Jamaicans living under colonial rule, the images were electric.

Two strange books circulated alongside the Bible. *The Holy Piby*, compiled by Robert Athlyi Rogers and published in Newark, New Jersey, in 1924, was a "Black man's Bible" that cast Garvey as an apostle. *The Royal Parchment Scroll of Black Supremacy*, by Fitz Balintine Pettersburg (1926), was a dense mystical text. Leonard Howell drew on both when writing *The Promised Key*, one of the earliest Rastafari tracts.

<div class="eyebrow">Pinnacle</div>
<h2 class="h-mid">The commune the police kept burning</h2>

Around 1940 Howell bought land at Pinnacle, near Sligoville in St Catherine, and built a self-sufficient community of several hundred followers. Residents farmed, refused to pay taxes to the colonial government, and grew cannabis as a cash crop and sacrament.

Police raided Pinnacle in 1941 and repeatedly afterwards. A raid in 1954 largely destroyed it, and police cleared the last residents in 1958. Its scattered residents moved into the poor districts of West Kingston, such as Back-O-Wall, where the movement grew, radicalised and became visible. Many scholars trace the first dreadlocks to this generation of young urban Rastas in the late 1940s and 1950s.

Pinnacle has since been protected as a heritage site, one outcome of the government's work on redress after Coral Gardens.
