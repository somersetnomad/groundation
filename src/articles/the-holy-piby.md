## A Bible for the children of Ethiopia

The *Holy Piby* was compiled by Robert Athlyi Rogers, an Anguillan preacher who died in 1931. He wrote it in the 1920s for the church he founded, the Afro-Athlican Constructive Gaathly, and it is usually dated to 1924.

Its central claim is that Ethiopians, meaning all Africans in the old biblical sense, are God's chosen people. The church taught self-reliance and self-determination for Black people, and the book was written as scripture for that purpose.

## What it contains

The *Piby* is arranged in four books:

- **The First Book of Athlyi, called Athlyi**, a short opening of two chapters
- **The Second Book of Athlyi, called Aggregation**, the longest, at fifteen chapters
- **The Third Book of Athlyi, named The Facts of the Apostles**
- **The Fourth Book of Athlyi, called Precaution**

A catechism of questions and answers follows, the kind of text a congregation could learn by heart.

## Garvey as apostle

The book's most striking move is to write Marcus Garvey into scripture. In the Second Book, Garvey is named one of three apostles of God. The catechism names him alongside Henrietta Vinton Davis and Robert Lincoln Poston, two leading figures of Garvey's UNIA, as saviours of the "down-trodden children of Ethiopia".

That matters because it shows how, before 1930, Garvey's political movement was already being understood in religious terms. When Haile Selassie was crowned, the frame was ready: Garvey could be the prophet, and Selassie the one he pointed towards.

## Why it matters to Rastafari

Scholars group three texts together as the root documents of Rastafari thought:

1. the *Holy Piby* (1924)
2. Fitz Balintine Pettersburg's *Royal Parchment Scroll of Black Supremacy* (1926)
3. Leonard Howell's *The Promised Key*, published under the name G. G. Maragh in the 1930s

The *Piby* is not itself a Rastafari book: it was written before the coronation and for another church. But Howell drew on it directly, and its language of chosen people, Black redemption and Garvey's holy mission runs through the earliest Rasta preaching.

## Reading it today

The text circulates widely online and in small reprints. Read it as a historical document, alongside a guide such as Robert A. Hill's work on early Rastafari, rather than as a statement of what Rastas believe now. Most Rastafari today read the King James Bible, not the *Piby*.
