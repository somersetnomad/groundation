## The crowd

Word spread across Jamaica that the man many Rastas considered God was coming. On 21 April 1966, around 100,000 people made their way to Palisadoes Airport in Kingston. The day was overcast and stormy.

Haile Selassie's Ethiopian Airlines flight landed at 1:30 in the afternoon. Then nothing happened. The crowd pressed onto the tarmac, and for close to an hour the aircraft door stayed shut.

## Planno on the steps

Jamaican officials turned to the one person they thought could help: Mortimo Planno, a Rastafari elder from Trench Town. Planno climbed the steps to the aircraft. When he came out again, he told the crowd that the Emperor wanted them to be calm. Then he walked down the steps with Selassie beside him.

Press photographs of the two men descending together made Planno a legend. Planno was also a teacher and friend to the young Bob Marley, and a few years later he would finance the session for Marley's first openly Rastafarian song.

Selassie waved from the steps; some who were there say he wept, though that is still debated. He did not walk the red carpet laid out for him. Rastas say the Emperor's feet touched Jamaican ground that day, and that is where the name **Grounation Day** comes from.

## Rita Marley's conversion

Bob Marley was in the United States at the time. His wife, Rita, watched the motorcade pass. She later said she saw the mark of a stigma on Selassie's hand, and that from that moment she was convinced he was divine.

## What the Emperor said

During the visit Selassie met Rastafari elders privately and gave gold medallions bearing the Ethiopian seal. He never rebuked them for calling him the Messiah.

He reportedly told the elders not to move to Ethiopia until they had first liberated the people of Jamaica. Rastas still quote the principle as **"liberation before repatriation"**: build justice at home first, then return.

## What changed

Before 1966 most of Jamaican society treated Rastas as outcasts, criminals or madmen. Three years after Coral Gardens, the sight of a head of state honouring them in public changed that. Rastafari gained a kind of respectability for the first time, and many historians link that shift to reggae's rise in the years that followed.

In 1970 Selassie sent Archbishop Laike Mandefro to establish an Ethiopian Orthodox mission in Jamaica. Around 2,000 Rastas were baptised into the church, a step that still divides the movement.

Today 21 April is one of the most important days in the Rastafari calendar, marked with Nyabinghi drumming and chanting.
