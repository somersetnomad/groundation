## A gift in return for loyalty

When Mussolini invaded Ethiopia in 1935, Black communities across the Americas rallied to its defence. In New York in August 1937, Dr Malaku Bayen, the Emperor's cousin and personal physician, founded the **Ethiopian World Federation** with his wife Dorothy to organise that support. Its first branch outside the United States opened in Kingston.

In 1948, in thanks, Haile Selassie granted the Federation five gashas of land near Shashamane, about 200 hectares or 500 acres, in the Rift Valley south of Addis Ababa. It was meant for people of the African diaspora who wanted to come home to Africa.

## Who came

Most of the earliest settlers to take up the offer were African Americans. Jamaicans began arriving in the 1960s, and they came in such numbers that the settlement picked up the nickname **"Little Jamaica"**. Rastafari of different houses, including members of the Twelve Tribes of Israel, built homes, farms and gathering places there.

Life was hard. The first pioneers were poor, the land was unfamiliar, and they arrived with few possessions. Few photographs of those early years survive.

## The Derg

In 1974 a military committee known as the Derg overthrew Haile Selassie, and in 1975 it nationalised land across Ethiopia. Much of the Shashamane grant was taken. The settlers stayed, but the land they had been promised was no longer theirs, and later arrivals found that Ethiopian law no longer allowed land to pass to foreign citizens.

## Stateless at home

The deeper problem was citizenship. Ethiopian nationality does not pass simply by being born in the country, so the children and grandchildren of settlers were not Ethiopian. Many were not fully Jamaican or American either. By 2001 around 200 Rastafari families lived in Shashamane, some in their third generation, without secure legal status.

## The 2017 decision

On 27 July 2017 Ethiopia announced identity cards for close to 1,000 Rastafari living mainly in Shashamane and Addis Ababa. The cards let holders enter Ethiopia without a visa and live there without a residence permit.

They are not citizenship. Holders still cannot vote or work in the security and defence sectors. Community members welcomed the move as long overdue recognition, and many continue to push for full citizenship.

## Shashamane today

Shashamane is still home to a Rastafari community, along with churches, small businesses and a steady stream of visitors from the diaspora. It remains the most concrete form the dream of repatriation has ever taken, and a reminder of how complicated that dream becomes on the ground.
