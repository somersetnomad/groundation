## After Pinnacle

When police broke up the commune at Pinnacle in the 1950s, many of its people came down from the hills into West Kingston. One of the places they settled was **Back-O-Wall**, an informal settlement of shacks and yards near the city's waterfront.

It was among the poorest places in the Caribbean. By the 1950s well over 5,000 people lived there, sharing just three communal standpipes and two public bathrooms. It was also home to the poor, the unemployed and, in the eyes of the authorities, criminals. To the state, it was a slum. To many Rastas, it was where the movement became a community.

## A gathering place

Back-O-Wall was where Rastafari grew loud and visible. Yards there hosted reasonings, drumming and the militant young Rastas of the 1940s and 1950s, many linked to the House of Youth Black Faith.

In March 1958 Back-O-Wall hosted the first Rastafari Universal Convention, a mass gathering of Rastas from across the island. Mortimo Planno, a leading elder, helped bring it about, and it was the setting in which Charles Edwards emerged as Prince Emmanuel, founder of the Bobo Shanti.

## The removals

Plans to redevelop the area went back to the 1950s. After independence, the work was taken forward with Edward Seaga, elected for the Jamaica Labour Party in 1963 and then minister of development, as the area's representative.

The redevelopment came in phases. In 1963, 932 families, more than 3,600 people, were removed from the nearby Foreshore Road area to make way for the first phase. Then, between February and July 1966, about 2,000 people were moved out of Back-O-Wall itself to build the second and third phases of the new housing scheme: **Tivoli Gardens**.

The removals came the same year as Haile Selassie's visit. Within months of the Emperor's welcome at Palisadoes, one of the movement's best-known homes was gone.

## What replaced it

Tivoli Gardens was presented as a model modern community. Critics said the rehousing also reshaped the area's politics, as supporters of one party moved out and supporters of the other moved in. The anthropologist Deborah A. Thomas describes Tivoli as a "garrison community", where political loyalty is exchanged for contracts and welfare, managed through a politician and a local strongman.

In later decades Tivoli became a centre of armed conflict, with major confrontations between gunmen and security forces in 1997, 2001, 2005, 2008 and 2010.

## Why remember it

Back-O-Wall is one of the many places where Rastafari history was simply built over. There is no monument there. Remembering it is part of the same work as remembering Pinnacle and Coral Gardens: recognising how often the movement's homes were taken from it.
