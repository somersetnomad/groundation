## A long way round

Leonard Percival Howell was born on 16 June 1898 in upper Clarendon, Jamaica, the eldest of ten children. He left the island young and spent around twenty years abroad. He worked as a seaman, lived for a time in Panama, and travelled back and forth to New York.

In New York he joined Marcus Garvey's Universal Negro Improvement Association and met Garvey himself. He was jailed in the United States over his UNIA involvement and eventually deported. When he came home in December 1932, at 34, he brought with him two decades of Black nationalist politics and a new conviction about the Emperor crowned in Addis Ababa two years earlier.

## "Not the King of England"

In January 1933, at Redemption Ground in Kingston, Howell publicly declared Haile Selassie divine. Few people listened at first. He moved east to St Thomas, the parish of the 1865 Morant Bay rebellion, and began holding meetings in Trinity Ville, Port Morant and Seaforth. His message was simple and, to the colonial authorities, dangerous: Black Jamaicans owed their loyalty to Selassie, not to King George V.

Police watched the meetings and called them seditious. In January 1934 Howell and his associate Robert Hinds were arrested. At his trial in March 1934 Howell pleaded not guilty and defended himself. He was sentenced to two years for sedition.

## The Promised Key

Howell's tract *The Promised Key* appeared under the pen name G. G. Maragh. It drew on two earlier texts, the *Holy Piby* and the *Royal Parchment Scroll of Black Supremacy*, and it was fiercely hostile to the colonial church. It became one of the founding documents of Rastafari thought.

The state's response went beyond prison. In 1938 Howell was certified insane over the book's content and sent to Bellevue Asylum in Kingston. Labelling Rastafari as madness, rather than belief, would become a pattern.

## Pinnacle

In 1940 Howell founded Pinnacle, on a hilltop near Sligoville in St Catherine. It was a self-sustaining community of followers who farmed, built their own homes and lived apart from the colonial order. Police raided it in 1941, calling it a "communist experiment", and Howell was again jailed for sedition until 1943.

Pinnacle survived for years more. In 1954 it was largely destroyed, and a final police raid in 1958 cleared the remaining residents. Many went to West Kingston, where the movement grew louder and more visible.

## The last years

Accounts of Howell's later life are disputed. Some say he withdrew from public life around 1958; historians point to court records showing him still fighting over Pinnacle's ownership. Months before his death he was attacked and beaten at Tredegar Park, near Pinnacle. He died in Kingston on 23 January 1981.

For decades he was barely mentioned in Jamaican history. Today Pinnacle is a protected heritage site, and Howell is increasingly honoured as the movement's first prophet.
